How to Declutter Your Home in Just 15 Minutes a Day

By the Everyday Simple Living Team

Most decluttering advice fails for the same reason most crash diets fail: it asks you to change everything at once. "Take a whole weekend and empty every closet!" sounds heroic, but two hours into a mountain of belongings, most people give up — and the house looks worse than when they started.

The 15-minute method works differently. Instead of one dramatic overhaul, you make small, steady progress every day. Fifteen minutes is short enough that you never feel dread, and long enough to make a visible difference in any room. Here's how to do it.

The basic method

Each day, choose one small surface or area — a single drawer, one shelf, the kitchen counter, your bedside table. Set a timer for fifteen minutes. When it rings, stop. Even if you're on a roll, stop. Stopping while you still feel good is what makes you want to come back tomorrow.

The three-box rule

As you work through your area, sort everything into three categories:

  • Keep — things you use, need, or truly love. Return these to a designated "home."
  • Out — things to donate, sell, or recycle. Don't agonize; be reasonably decisive.
  • Elsewhere — items that belong in another room. Carry them home when the timer ends.

Three boxes, one timer, one area. That's the whole system. No special equipment, no expensive organizers.

A sample two-week plan

  • Day 1: One kitchen drawer
  • Day 2: Kitchen countertop
  • Day 3: Bathroom cabinet
  • Day 4: One bookshelf shelf
  • Day 5: Nightstand
  • Day 6: Entry table and shoe area
  • Day 7: Rest — admire your progress
  • Week 2: Repeat with closets, one shelf at a time

Four rules that make decluttering easier

1. Ask "would I buy this again today?"

Instead of "might I need this someday?", ask whether, if you saw this item in a shop today at its current price, you'd buy it. "Might need it someday" is how drawers fill up with cable chargers for phones you no longer own.

2. Clear flat surfaces first

Counters, tables, and desks visually define how tidy a home feels. A room with clear flat surfaces looks organized even if the closet behind the door is chaotic. Prioritize surfaces for maximum visible payoff.

3. Give every item a home

Clutter is usually homeless belongings, not excess belongings. When everything has a designated spot, tidying becomes a 30-second reset instead of a project. If an item has no home, either assign one or admit it's an "Out."

4. Handle the "Out" box immediately

Donations that sit by the door for three months become furniture. Put the "Out" box in your car the same day you finish a session, and drop it off within the week.

What to do about sentimental items

Sentimental clutter is the hardest category, and the 15-minute method handles it perfectly: give it a dedicated slot — maybe every seventh session — and keep the timer. When time is limited, you naturally keep the most meaningful items and let go of the rest. A shoebox of carefully chosen keepsakes honors memories better than an attic of forgotten boxes ever could.

The quiet payoff

After two or three weeks of 15-minute sessions, something noticeable happens: your home starts to feel lighter, cleaning takes less time, and you stop losing things. That's the real benefit of decluttering — not a picture-perfect house, but a home that's easier to live in.

Start today with one drawer. Fifteen minutes. That's all it takes.
